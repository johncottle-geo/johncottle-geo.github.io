Research

Himalayan Tectonics

Mid-crustal deformation, melting and exhumation in the Himalaya-Tibet system.

How does the middle crust of a continental collision deform, melt and return to the surface? The Himalaya is our main natural test of whether the exposed middle crust moved as a weak, flowing channel or was stacked as a series of distinct thrust sheets, and our dating shows that the answer changes in time and along strike.

Assembling the metamorphic core

Our hypothesis is that the Himalayan metamorphic core was built from several tectonic slices that were metamorphosed at different times.

  • Discontinuities within the mid-crust separate slices with different histories, and the Main Central Thrust is better described as a system than as a single fault. Larson and Cottle 2014, Tectonics; Larson et al. 2015, Earth and Planetary Science Letters; Cottle, Larson and Kellett 2015, Journal of Structural Geology
  • The core records several metamorphic episodes, and the thrust belt that assembled it can be reconstructed slice by slice. Lihter et al. 2022, Geology; Shrestha et al. 2020, Tectonics
  • Monazite and titanite petrochronology separates pulses of metamorphism along strike. Mottram, Cottle and Kylander-Clark 2018, Geoscience Frontiers; Cottle, Lederer and Larson 2019, Chemical Geology

Timing of melting, cooling and exhumation

  • Melting in the Himalayan middle crust lasted for millions of years, with the timescales set by accessory mineral growth and breakdown. Lederer et al. 2013, Contributions to Mineralogy and Petrology; Cottle, Larson and Yakymchuk 2018, Lithos
  • In east-central Nepal and the Khumbu Himal, anatexis, cooling and exhumation occurred in Miocene pulses. Larson et al. 2016, Geosphere; Larson et al. 2021, International Geology Review
  • Geochronology of the upper Greater Himalayan sequence and of the Everest region ties melting to deformation along the South Tibetan Detachment. Cottle et al. 2009, Journal of Geology; Larson, Cottle and Godin 2011, GSA Lithosphere

Along-strike variation and out-of-sequence deformation

  • Metamorphism and exhumation were diachronous along the range, controlled in part by reactivated basement structures. Braden, Godin and Cottle 2017, Lithos; Morrell et al. 2024, GSA Bulletin
  • Renewed ductile thrusting after 8 Ma in western Nepal, and early Oligocene onset of the South Tibetan Detachment, show how the system evolved in time. Braden et al. 2018, Geology; Soucy La Roche et al. 2016, Geology
  • Klippen preserve the early evolution of the middle crust. Soucy La Roche et al. 2018, Tectonics

Orogen-parallel extension and gneiss domes

  • East-west extension began in the mid-Miocene and developed progressively across the Tibetan plateau. Larson et al. 2019, Terra Nova; Brubacher et al. 2020, International Geology Review
  • The Ama Drime Massif exposes Eocene deep crust that records the early evolution of the orogen. Cottle et al. 2009, Tectonics; Kellett, Cottle and Smit 2014, GSA Lithosphere
  • The Leo Pargil and Lhagoi Kangri domes show the timing of Barrovian metamorphism, melting, ductile deformation and decompression in the doming mid-crust. Langille et al. 2012, Journal of Metamorphic Geology; Cottle, Diedesch and Jessup 2025, Journal of the Geological Society; Cottle et al. 2025, Journal of the Geological Society

Mount Everest and the wider collision

  • The Everest massif combines channel flow and low-angle normal faulting inside a compressional orogen, and its thermal history is now well constrained. Searle et al. 2024, Journal of the Geological Society; Larson et al. 2025, Journal of the Geological Society
  • Himalayan collision stems from subduction, and the Hindu Kush records terrane accretion that preceded it. Soret et al. 2021, Geology; Faisal et al. 2014, Terra Nova
